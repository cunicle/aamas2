# AAMAS 2027 — Submission 1241 — Reviewer 1

**Title:** Acting in Parallel Without Talking: Focal Points, Collisions, and Length Signals in Diffusion-LM Agents and LLM Agent Teams

**Reviewer profile:** senior PC member; multiagent coordination, game theory, focal points and conventions, ad hoc teamwork, teams of LLM agents.

---

## 1. Summary

The paper asks how parallel decisions that cannot see one another pick distinct roles. The test bed is a diffusion LLM (Dream-7B; LLaDA2.0-mini as a block-wise control) that fills the value slots of an *oracle* JSON skeleton for the 400 parallel / parallel_multiple requests of BFCL v4. Each slot is treated as a "decider", and the denoising schedule (k tokens per step, left-to-right, confidence threshold tau = 0.9) sets which deciders move together. The slots are compared with teams of 7B agents (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, or Dream decoding one token per step). In these teams each agent makes one call: at the same time or in turns, and anonymous, numbered, numbered and told an ordering rule, or "position agents" that get the whole skeleton and fill only call i. Three hypotheses are tested:

- **P1:** the request's mention order coordinates same-step slots. Committing 16 tokens per step instead of 1 costs Dream 5.3 points of set accuracy. Numbered simultaneous agents reach 28.8% (Dream) and 0% (Qwen), told-the-rule agents 48.1% and 34.6%, and Dream position agents 85.6%.
- **P2:** symmetric deciders collide. On 60 "choose any n of these cities" requests, simultaneous agents collide in 45–100% of teams, also with sampling at T = 0.7. Turn-taking and the tau threshold avoid collisions.
- **P3:** slot length steers values. One surplus mask drops Dream from 89.8% to 15.5%. Swapped sibling lengths bind values to the wrong calls even at k = 1. A pre-written closing delimiter and a type mask, isolated by two interface variants, explain part of the effect.

The paper ends with an evaluation protocol: decode at k = 1 under the agent's own length policy before attributing cross-call errors to parallel decoding. It also gives design recommendations.

The work is careful and self-critical. It uses paired bootstrap intervals, exact McNemar tests with Holm correction over 48 tests, controls for the delimiter and type mask, and a complete supplement with raw records. I re-derived the key numbers from the raw records and found them correct (see Section 7, FACT). My concerns are mainly about what the multiagent framing adds and about how the canvas-vs-team comparison is interpreted.

## 2. Relevance to AAMAS

The relevance is partial. Two parts of the paper are squarely AAMAS material: the teams of LLM agents (Table 2), and the question of which coordination devices work for LLM-based deciders that cannot communicate (labels, conventions, salience, turn-taking). This matters to the growing multi-agent LLM community at AAMAS, and the negative result on prompted labels deserves to be known.

However, roughly 60% of the body is a study of slot-length bias in skeleton-constrained dLLM decoding: Section 6, Section 7, Section 8, Figure 3, Tables 3–4, and much of Table 1. This includes closing delimiters, type masks, logit biases and length estimation. That is an NLP decoding-interface topic with no multiagent content. The "deciders" of one canvas are output positions of a single network evaluated in a single forward pass. The authors themselves say the team-theoretic vocabulary is used "only for who moves together and who sees what" (p.3, §3.2), so it provides no analytical leverage. In its current balance, the paper would fit an NLP or ML venue (or a workshop on LLM agents) at least as well as the AAMAS main track. A version centred on the team experiments, with stronger MAS baselines and models, would fit AAMAS much better.

## 3. Strengths

- **S1. Clean, reproducible experimental design with honest reporting.** All raw records, prompts, scripts and summaries are released. Every number I checked against the records matches (Section 7). Results that contradict predictions are reported as such: for example, "P2 holds only in part" for the canvas, and "P3 holds in part".
- **S2. Careful statistics.** The paper uses paired bootstrap intervals over requests, exact McNemar tests, Holm correction over all 48 paired tests (I counted 48 rows in `stats.md`), and Wilson intervals for single rates. It flags which contrasts do not survive correction, including the LLaDA2.0 contrasts and the canvas duplicate test (Holm p = 0.059).
- **S3. Mechanism-isolating interface variants.** Moving the closing token into the slot and making slots format-tolerant are good controls. They turn a vague "length bias" into specific, separable causes: the delimiter for strings, and the type mask turning filler into content. The teacher-forced probe (Fig. 3c) is informative.
- **S4. Useful evaluation message.** "An error that persists with one token per step is not an error of parallel decoding" (p.8, §7) is a simple, correct and actionable attribution rule. Table 4 shows why it matters: estimated lengths at k = 1 produce more cross-call errors than exact lengths at k = 16 (6.5% vs 2.0%).
- **S5. Some genuinely MAS-relevant observations.**
  - Anonymous simultaneous LLM agents collide.
  - Sampling at T = 0.7 does not rescue them: Qwen agents still pick the first-listed city 99.0–99.8% of the time, which is primary rather than Schelling salience.
  - A confidence threshold acts as a self-detecting symmetry signal. Two symmetric choices are rarely confident at once (8 of 60 list requests ever co-commit, which I verified from the traces), so tau serializes exactly the choices that need it.
  - Exact slot lengths act as an unintended private label: anonymous Dream agents reach 48.5% on the swap requests vs 0.6% for anonymous Qwen agents.
- **S6. Fairness controls in the comparison.** The team-symmetric subset (identical skeletons and lengths across siblings) and the 86 one-tool requests are sensible attempts to equalize what canvas slots and agents know. Comparing numbered agents (k = 1 within the call) with the canvas at k = 16 is conservative for the canvas. I also checked the authors' claim that canvas siblings do not see each other on the 86 one-tool requests. At k = 16, in 85 of 86 requests every call makes its first commitment in the same step, and 84.8% of the 607 sibling pairs commit their first tokens together. The claim holds.

## 4. Weaknesses (most serious first)

### W1. The multiagent framing is mostly vocabulary; the core coordination result reduces to confident marginals of one network (relevance, framing)

- **Where:** p.3, §3.2.
- **Quote:** "The deciders are positions of one network: they share one policy and differ only in their position on the canvas … Unlike in team theory, the policy is fixed by training and never optimized for this payoff; we use the terms only for who moves together and who sees what, and the predictions below are hypotheses, not consequences of the model."

Within a denoising step, every "decider" conditions on exactly the same canvas. In Radner's or Witsenhausen's terms the information structure is therefore not decentralized within a step. All members have identical information and differ only by an index (their position). What is decentralized is only the *sampling*: same-step tokens are drawn from factorized marginals. This is precisely the known parallel-decoding (copula) problem the paper cites ([22]).

The paper's own numbers support this simpler explanation of P1. The tau = 0.9 sampler commits 93.7% of sibling pairs together and still keeps 89.0% set accuracy (Table 1; `co_commit.md`). So the slots "coordinate" because the network's per-position marginals are already confident and position-specific, not because of anything a team-decision analysis predicts. P1–P3 are explicitly "hypotheses, not consequences of the model". No result in the paper uses the team-theory, focal-point or dispersion-game machinery beyond naming.

For AAMAS, I would like to see either a formal contribution or a sharper explanation of what the MAS lens predicts that a decoding analysis does not. A formal contribution could be, for example, a condition on marginal entropy or total correlation under which simultaneous commitment is safe, connecting tau to a confidence-gated protocol.

### W2. The canvas-vs-team comparison, and especially the "position agents", is over-interpreted

- **Where (claim):** p.5, §4.
- **Quote:** "For the same model and the same lack of information about the others, a role given as a position works and a role given as a number does not."
- **Where (abstract, p.1):** "while the same agents given their position on the whole skeleton reach 85.6%".
- **Where (Conclusion, p.8):** "Designers should give parallel deciders their roles through structure".

**(a) Dream position agents are not "given their position".** In the code, a position agent receives the request unchanged, with no note (`position_agent_example` in `ptcdiag/data/agents.py`). Its canvas is the whole turn. The decoder freezes the other calls' slots (`frozen` in `ptcdiag/decoding/constraints.py`, ~l. 513). Consequently the first forward pass is identical for all n agents, and agent i simply commits the most confident token among call i's positions. The role is imposed by which marginals the decoder reads, not communicated to the model.

The paper concedes the consequence for choose-N: "Dream's choose exactly as its canvas at 16 tokens per step does in all 105 requests, since they see the same canvas" (p.6, §5). What the 85.6% shows is that a model asked to predict the whole joint action assigns entities consistently across positions, and each agent can take its own part. In MAS terms this is coordination through a commonly computed joint plan: "each agent computes the joint plan and executes its part" (cf. Genesereth et al. [11], and social laws [39]). It is not evidence that "a position" is a better *role cue* than "a number".

**(b) "The same lack of information" is not accurate.** With greedy decoding and a shared deterministic policy, an agent that receives the common input can recompute the others' choices. The "without talking" constraint is then not binding. The obvious protocol is that each agent decodes the whole turn and keeps call i. This matches the single-agent result by construction (Dream canvas k = 1: 88.5%; Qwen AR: 91.3% on the 104 requests). It is the natural upper baseline for "position agents" and is missing. The only setting where decentralization truly bites is private randomness (T = 0.7) or private information, and the position-agent protocol was not run there.

**(c) Numbered agents differ from canvas slots in at least four ways at once:**

1. a single-call skeleton instead of the whole turn;
2. a natural-language note appended to the request;
3. an answer format (one call to a request that names n entities) that contradicts the system prompt. The system prompt says "If the question needs several calls, include all of them in the array" (`PROMPTS.md`).
4. a different within-call decoding schedule (k = 1 vs k = 16).

The 86-request argument controls only for (1). The result is consistent with "7B models follow output-format priors over NL instructions", which is a narrower and less MAS-relevant claim than "a role given as a number does not work".

**(d) The position cue is architecture-dependent.** Qwen position agents reach 54.8%. 43 of 289 copy the placeholder `"..."`/`null` that the AR interface teacher-forces into the other calls (`PLACEHOLDER` in `constraints.py`, l. 178). On choose-N, 188 of 210 non-first Qwen agents copy it, so the protocol was effectively not tested on symmetric requests for the AR model. This conflicts with p.8, §9: "The protocols and role cues behind P1 and P2 do not depend on diffusion".

**For the record:** on the 86 one-tool requests, Dream position agents reach 84.9% vs 32.6% for numbered agents (45 vs 0 discordant requests). This is the cleanest version of the comparison and is not reported.

### W3. The test of labels and conventions is narrow, and on symmetric requests mis-specified

- **Where:** p.6, §5.
- **Quote:** "Told the rule, Qwen2.5 and Dream agents still collide in 70.0% and 45.0% of list teams … although the rule's wording, which refers to the order of the calls, may fit a list of cities poorly."
- **Then the same section:** "P2 holds for teams of 7B agents: a number in the prompt, a stated rule, or sampling leaves 45–100% of simultaneous list teams colliding".

The rule ("assistant i makes the i-th of the calls, in the order in which the request mentions them") is ill-posed for a list of cities, since the request mentions cities, not calls. So the list-team result cannot be counted as evidence that "a stated rule" fails.

More generally:

- There is one wording per protocol and only two 7B models (acknowledged on p.8, §9).
- No prompt tells agents what focal reasoning needs: that teammates are identical copies of the same model receiving the same text except the number.
- On BFCL, rule-following degrades sharply with team size. Dream told the rule reaches 63.2% / 33.3% / 29.2% for n = 2 / 3 / 4; Qwen reaches 47.4% / 28.6% / 12.5% (`review_numbers.md`, teams_by). Dream position agents reach 84.2% / 95.2% / 83.3%. This suggests ordinal parsing or instruction-following limits of 7B models rather than anything about labels as such.

Under these conditions, the design advice in the conclusion is not established beyond these models and this wording. The conclusion says "Designers should give parallel deciders their roles through structure", and contribution (ii) says "a number or a rule in a 7B agent's prompt mostly does not".

### W4. "Focal points" in the title vs. what is shown; missing theoretical benchmarks; tautological rows

- **Where:** Title; p.6, §5.
- **Quote:** "What the agents share is primary salience, the choice each finds salient for itself, rather than a choice made to match the others".
- **Also p.6, §5:** "The canvas of Section 4 fits the same description".

By the paper's own analysis, no Schelling (secondary) salience or focal-point *reasoning* is observed. The coordination that occurs comes from a shared primary-salience prior plus position. The ordered task is an assignment (anti-coordination) problem, and "order coordinates" amounts to "the model reproduces the order it was trained to emit". The title and the introduction ("a focal point that all of them notice … We ask which of these work") promise more than this.

Two benchmark issues:

- **Sampled teams.** The right benchmark for anonymous symmetric randomization is the symmetric mixed strategy (uniform over the n+3 cities: 43.2% collisions, computed by the authors). It is used only for the canvas. I checked the sampled anonymous teams. They collide at exactly the rate predicted by independent draws from their own pooled per-agent city distribution: Dream 87.7% observed vs 87.8% predicted, Qwen 99.7% vs 99.7%. So the failure is entirely insufficient randomness of the individual policy, far from the 43.2% that even optimal symmetric randomization would leave. That is the MAS-relevant statement, and it is not made.
- **Deterministic canvas.** Comparing a deterministic greedy policy to "chance" ("more often than chance", p.5) is a category mismatch. The relevant deterministic baseline is the 100% collision of identical inputs.

The "same time, anonymous" rows of Table 2 (0.0% / 100%) are tautological: identical inputs to a deterministic policy give identical outputs. Anonymous symmetric processes cannot break symmetry deterministically (Angluin 1980). These rows are sanity checks, not evidence for P2. Turn-taking agents observe earlier calls, so "turn-taking prevents collisions" is expected. It is coordination through observed actions, not without communication.

### W5. Gaps in the MAS related work

- **Where:** p.2, §2, "Coordination without communication".

The focal-point literature is well covered (Schelling, Fenster/Kraus/Rosenschein, Mehta/Starmer/Sugden, Bardsley et al., dispersion games). Missing are the lines of work that most directly predict or contextualize the results:

- **Decentralized control and information structures:** Dec-POMDPs (Bernstein et al. 2002; Oliehoek & Amato 2016) and Witsenhausen's information structures (1971). These are the standard formalism for "who sees what" at AAMAS.
- **Symmetry breaking among anonymous identical processes and renaming:** Angluin 1980; Attiya et al. 1990. These predict the anonymous-team rows exactly.
- **Emergence of conventions in MAS:** Shoham & Tennenholtz 1997; Walker & Wooldridge 1995.
- **Role assignment by formation position in teams:** Stone & Veloso 1999 ("locker-room agreement"). This is the closest precedent to "position as role". Also multi-robot task allocation (Gerkey & Matarić 2004).
- **Agent-ID inputs to shared policies in MARL:** Terry et al. 2020; ROMA, Wang et al. 2020. These are directly relevant to "deciders share one policy and get their roles only through their inputs". Only Christianos et al. [8] is cited.
- **Role-prompted multi-agent LLM frameworks:** CAMEL, MetaGPT, AutoGen, ChatDev. These give roles in prompts, which is exactly the practice the negative result speaks to.

### W6. Null results stated as equivalence with tests that cannot reject

- **Where:** p.5, §4.
- **Quote:** "Turn-taking agents do not differ significantly from one agent making all calls … (p ≥ 0.125)".
- **Also:** position agents are "not significantly different from its canvas".

With 3–5 discordant requests, the exact McNemar p-value cannot fall below 0.125. Meanwhile the paper's own bootstrap intervals exclude zero: Dream turn-anon vs canvas k = 1 is 3.8 points [1.0, 7.7] (4 vs 0), and position agents vs canvas k = 16 is 3.8 points [1.0, 7.7] (4 vs 0) (`stats.md`). Absence of significance at this power is not evidence of equivalence. The "within 4 points" wording in the abstract is fine; the "do not differ" wording is not.

### W7. CCER treats unparsed outputs as free of cross-call errors; open choose-N columns are close to uninformative

- **Where:** p.8, §7.
- **Quote:** "Surplus masks mostly corrupt single values (Dream's CCER with one extra mask: 2.8%, not significantly above 2.0%)".

CCER counts only parsed outputs as possible cross-call errors, but parse rates differ strongly by condition. From the raw records, unparsed rates are:

| Condition | Unparsed |
|---|---|
| Dream exact, k = 16 | 5.2% |
| +1, k = 1 | 17.8% |
| +2, k = 1 | 24.8% |
| Estimate, k = 1 | 10.5% |
| Estimate, k = 16 | 18.0% |

So null CCER differences under surplus masks are biased toward zero. The main Section 7 contrasts are conservative under this bias, but the "surplus mostly corrupts single values" conclusion is not.

**Open choose-N columns (Table 2, p.5).** Greedy Dream at k = 16 gives three distinct outputs over all 45 open requests (["Chicago","Chicago"], ["Chicago"×3], ["Chicago"×3,"Houston"], from `dream/choose.jsonl`). The 100.0% entry is therefore effectively three observations. Qwen and LLaDA2.0 outputs contain an invalid (cut) city in 100% of open requests (`choose.md`). The caption does not say this.

### W8. The oracle skeleton assumes away the MAS question

- **Where:** p.3, §3.1 ("Our skeleton is an oracle, built from the reference calls") and p.8, §9.

The skeleton fixes the number of calls, their tools and their order, and lays the calls out in reference order, which mostly matches mention order. For a team, this skeleton *is* the shared plan that an orchestrator would have to produce. The canvas's and position agents' success is therefore conditional on someone having already solved the role-assignment structure. The authors acknowledge that systems with a shared plan "resemble our canvas and position agents more than our numbered teams; we did not test them". For an AAMAS paper this is the central untested case: who writes the plan, and what happens when it is wrong.

### W9. Clarity and documentation

- The paper is very dense. Table 2 alone mixes three metrics, two decoding temperatures and eighteen rows, and many conditions (tau, LTR, tolerant, closer-in-slot, one slot longer, estimate) appear in single sentences.
- The paper says "the supplementary material gives all texts" (p.3, §3.2), and the supplement README says `PROMPTS.md` holds "Every prompt text". But `PROMPTS.md` lists only the five notes. The position-agent input and the placeholder strings (`"..."` and `" null"`) that Qwen position agents copy appear only in code.
- Two supplement summaries disagree on the "one slot longer" sibling-fit share. `closer.csv`, used for Table 3, gives 35.4% / 14.0%; `swap_direction.md` gives 37.0% / 14.8%. The paper matches `closer.csv`.

## 5. Questions for the authors

1. Can you confirm that the n Dream position agents share an identical first forward pass and differ only in which positions may be committed? If so, how do you distinguish "a position acts as a role" from "each agent reads its part of a jointly predicted turn"?
2. Why is the replicated-joint-plan protocol (each agent decodes the whole turn and keeps call i) not included? It is the textbook decentralized-execution baseline, and under T = 0.7 with private seeds it would measure the real cost of private randomness.
3. Did you try any paraphrases of the numbered and told-the-rule notes? Did you try a note stating that all assistants are identical copies receiving the same text except their number, which is the premise for reasoning about others' decision procedures?
4. How do numbered agents perform when they receive the whole skeleton *and* their number? This would separate cue modality from input format.
5. Why was the choose-N rule not stated as "assistant i takes the i-th listed city"?
6. Do the team results hold for a larger instruction-tuned model (e.g., 32B–72B)? The team experiments are cheap (104 + 60 requests).
7. Is CCER over parsed outputs, or a slot-level cross-call classification of unparsed outputs, consistent with the Section 7 conclusions?
8. Is there a formal statement behind the observation that tau serializes symmetric choices, for example a bound relating the collision probability of same-step commitments to the marginals' confidence? Could tau be turned into a confidence-gated *team* protocol (act now if confident, otherwise wait for earlier agents)?
9. In Section 4, "most numbered agents take the entity mentioned first": is this measured against the mention order or the reference order (`first_mention` in `agents_bfcl.md` uses the reference call)?

**Justification.** This is a careful, honest and reproducible empirical study. Its numbers check out against the raw records, and its evaluation message (S4) is useful. For the AAMAS main track, however:

- the multiagent contribution is a thin slice of the paper (W1);
- its headline interpretation (position vs number) rests on a comparison that changes several things at once, and on "position agents" whose role is imposed by the decoder (W2);
- the convention test is narrow and partly mis-specified (W3);
- the focal-point framing is not borne out by the paper's own salience analysis (W4);
- the MAS related work misses the formal frameworks that predict several results (W5).

A version centred on teams of LLM agents could be a clear accept at AAMAS. It would need a replicated-joint-plan baseline, correctly posed conventions, prompt paraphrases, a larger model, and the length analysis condensed.
