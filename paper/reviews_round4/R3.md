"Acting in Parallel Without Talking: Focal Points, Collisions, and Length Signals in Diffusion-LM Agents and LLM Agent Teams"

# PART A: Review

## Summary

The paper asks how parallel decisions that cannot see each other end up taking distinct roles. It studies two kinds of "deciders":

1. **The value slots of one diffusion-LM (dLLM) agent.** The agent fills an oracle JSON "skeleton" of a parallel tool-calling turn. Slots committed in the same denoising step are chosen without seeing each other. The parameter k (tokens per step) sets who moves together.
2. **Teams of 7B LLM agents** (Qwen2.5-7B, or Dream-7B decoding one token per step). Each agent makes one call. Agents act at the same time or in turns, and are anonymous, numbered, numbered and told the rule "agent i makes the i-th call", or given their position on the whole skeleton ("position agents").

There are three hypotheses: P1 the request's mention order coordinates same-step slots; P2 symmetric deciders that move together collide; P3 slot length (the number of masks) steers the value. They are tested on 400 BFCL parallel requests, 105 synthetic "choose-N" weather requests, and a battery of length interventions (surplus, swap, one slot longer, model-estimated lengths, closing delimiter moved into the slot, format-tolerant type mask).

Main findings:
- Going from k=1 to k=16 costs Dream only 5.3 points of set accuracy.
- Simultaneous numbered Dream agents reach 28.8% (48.1% told the rule), against 81.7% for the canvas. Dream position agents reach 85.6%.
- Turn-taking teams come within 4 points of one agent.
- On symmetric requests, simultaneous agents collide even when numbered, told the rule, or sampled.
- Wrong slot lengths corrupt values and rebind them to the wrong calls even at k=1. Part of this is due to the pre-written closing delimiter and the type mask.
- With model-estimated lengths, k=1 produces more cross-call errors than k=16 with exact lengths. Hence an attribution recipe: decode at k=1 under the agent's own length policy before blaming parallel decoding.

## Strengths

- **S1. Careful, well-controlled interventions.** The design isolates one factor at a time:
  - the k sweep, the threshold and LTR decoding;
  - swap versus "one slot longer";
  - the closing delimiter moved into the slot;
  - the format-tolerant type mask;
  - the 86 one-tool requests as a control for "agents see only their own call";
  - position agents as a control for "number versus position".

  These controls answer the obvious objections one by one.
- **S2. Honest, careful statistics.** Paired McNemar tests with bootstrap CIs, Holm's correction over all 48 paired tests, Wilson intervals for single rates, and explicit statements when a result does not survive correction (e.g., the canvas duplicates in Sec. 5, Holm p = 0.059). Hypotheses are reported as holding "in part" when they do (P2 for the canvas, P3).
- **S3. An actionable, practically important observation.** In skeleton dLLM interfaces, length artefacts produce errors that look exactly like parallel-decoding coordination failures (bound values, duplicates), and they persist at k=1. The Sec. 7 recipe is simple and useful for anyone evaluating dLLM agents.
- **S4. A useful design lesson for LLM multi-agent systems.** For 7B models, a role given as a number or a stated rule in the prompt is weak, and a role given as a position in a shared structure works. This is relevant to orchestrator/worker designs, and it echoes the "agent indication" problem in parameter-sharing MARL.
- **S5. Excellent figures and supplement.**
  - Figure 1 is a model teaser. A verbatim request shows three outcomes, and the reader understands the length cue in ten seconds.
  - Figure 2 is a clear map of the test bed.
  - The supplement has code, all prompts, raw records, a reproduction script and expected outputs that match the paper. The AI-use statement is transparent.

## Weaknesses (most serious first)

**W1. The multi-agent contribution is thin relative to the framing (Secs. 1, 3.2, 4, 5).**
- The title and introduction promise focal points and team decision problems. But the paper itself says the team-theory vocabulary is used "only for who moves together and who sees what, and the predictions below are hypotheses, not consequences of the model" (Sec. 3.2, p.3).
- Focal points are never analysed beyond the observation that agents show "primary salience, the choice each finds salient for itself" (Sec. 5, p.6).
- The headline team result for anonymous simultaneous agents is true by construction: "Anonymous agents that act at the same time receive identical inputs and make identical calls" (Sec. 4, p.5). Greedy decoding of identical inputs is deterministic.
- The non-trivial team results (numbered 28.8%, rule 48.1%, position 85.6%) come from one prompt wording per protocol and 7B models (Sec. 9). So they speak more to 7B instruction following than to coordination.
- About half of the paper's substance (Secs. 6–8, plus Tables 3, 4 and Figure 3) concerns slot-length artefacts of a dLLM decoding interface. That is an NLP/decoding topic. For an AAMAS audience, the paper's significance rests on the "deciders" lens, which is applied loosely.

**W2. External validity and confounds in the key comparisons (Secs. 3.1, 4, 9).**
- **The oracle skeleton.** It fixes "the number of calls, their tools, their argument names, and their order" (Sec. 9), and it "lays the calls out in the reference order, which BFCL usually gives in mention order" (Sec. 4, p.5). So P1's support for the canvas is partly built in. The paper concedes "The slots rarely face a hard choice".
- **"A role given as a position works and a role given as a number does not" (Sec. 4, p.5).** This conflates the content of the cue (index i) with its format. A position agent's task is ordinary in-distribution completion of the i-th element of a JSON array. A numbered agent must map a natural-language index onto the request. No condition separates the two, e.g., a numbered agent whose call is marked inside the full skeleton, or a reworded rule.
- **Qwen2.5 position agents.** They see placeholders, which the code shows are "..." or null; the paper never says so. 43 of 289 agents copy them on BFCL, and 188 of 210 on choose-N. So Qwen's 54.8% is not a clean test of the position cue.
- **The canvas/team comparison mixes k.** The canvas runs at k=16 and Dream agents at k=1.

**W3. Clarity for a non-expert reader (throughout).**
- The prose is precise but very dense. Most sentences carry two to five numbers; the abstract alone has seven.
- Many terms are used before they are defined (see the table below). Examples: set accuracy, "told the rule", type mask, closing delimiter, sibling, cross-call error, confidence threshold, near miss, cost of parallelism.
- Several terms are inconsistent:
  - "chimera" (Sec. 3.4, Table 4) versus "splice/spliced" (Sec. 7);
  - "length cue" (body) versus "Length Signals" (title);
  - "protocol" means who-moves-together (Sec. 3.2) but also the "attribution protocol" (Sec. 1) and the "evaluation protocol" (Sec. 7);
  - "Order" means mention order in Table 1 and list order in Table 2.
- The difference between the 213 "equal-length" and the 104 "team-symmetric" requests is not explained. From the code (`length_symmetric`), requests in which no argument repeats count as equal-length.
- An unnamed fourth hypothesis follows "We test three hypotheses" (end of Sec. 3.3: "If a prompted number acts as a role, ...").
- Section 6 (the closing-delimiter variant, the probe, the type-mask filler, format-tolerant slots) was the hardest part for me. I needed several passes.

**W4. "No significant difference" is used as evidence of parity with very few discordant pairs (Sec. 4).**
- "Turn-taking agents do not differ significantly from one agent making all calls" and position agents are "not significantly different from its canvas" (p.5). These rest on exact McNemar tests with 3–4 versus 0–1 discordant requests out of 104.
- For several of these contrasts, the authors' own paired bootstrap intervals (supplement, `stats.md`; not printed in the paper) exclude zero:
  - Dream canvas k=1 versus turn-anonymous: 3.8 points [1.0, 7.7];
  - position versus canvas k=16: 3.8 [1.0, 7.7];
  - Dream CCER k=1 versus k=16: −1.0 [−2.0, −0.3], reported in the text as "(p = 0.13)".

  The two inference methods disagree here, and the text reports only the one that favours "no difference". The abstract's "come within 4 points" is accurate. "Do not differ significantly" invites a reading of equivalence the data cannot support.
- A related overstatement: contribution (ii) and Sec. 8 say turn-taking "prevents collisions on ordered ... requests". The supplement (`agents_bfcl.md`) shows Dream turn-taking teams with a duplicated call in 1 of 104 ordered requests. Table 2 does not report ordered-request duplicates, so the reader cannot check this.

**W5. Small samples for some claims, and preliminary mitigations (Secs. 5, 6, 8, 9).**
- P2 is tested on 60 list requests. The canvas duplicate rate (15.0%) does not survive Holm.
- LLaDA2.0's wrong-length runs use 100 requests and the swap uses 34. Only one of its cross-call contrasts survives correction, and it does not use its native tool format.
- In Sec. 8, β is tuned on the evaluation requests (acknowledged). The one-pass length estimator reaches 42.3% against 89.8% with exact lengths, and the two remedies are not combined.

  As a result, the design recommendations (Sec. 8) are less supported than the diagnostic findings.

**W6. Reproducibility gaps (minor).**
- Some numbers in Sec. 6 cannot be traced to the supplement's summaries, although the README maps "Other numbers" to them:
  - "lies outside the slot's type mask in 59.3% of slots";
  - "1,076 of 1,464";
  - "965 of 1,014 integer slots".
- Sec. 3.2 says "the supplementary material gives all texts", but PROMPTS.md does not show the position agents' input or placeholder text.
- `stats.md` gives the tolerant +1 accuracy as 28.2, while the paper gives 28.3 (113/400 = 28.25).
- AI_USE.md refers to "experiments B, C and D", labels the paper never defines.

**W7. Missing related work from MARL (Sec. 2).**
- Parameter-sharing MARL has studied the issue of "numbers as roles" directly. Agent-ID or "agent indication" inputs are needed for heterogeneous behaviour (e.g., Terry et al., "Revisiting Parameter Sharing in Multi-Agent Deep RL", 2020). Symmetry breaking in Dec-POMDPs with shared policies is also directly relevant.
- In MARL, IDs work because the policy is trained with them. The paper's frozen LLMs cannot exploit an ID zero-shot but can exploit a position that matches their training distribution. Saying this would sharpen the paper's own message for an AAMAS audience. Only [8] is cited.

## Questions to the authors

1. **Position versus number.** Can you separate "role carried by structure" from "role carried by natural language"? For example, a numbered agent that receives the full skeleton with its own call marked by a comment, or the rule worded as "fill the i-th element of the array". Without this, "positions coordinate, numbers do not" may simply mean "in-distribution formats work".
2. **Robustness of the team results.** Do they survive (a) two or three paraphrases of each protocol note and (b) a larger model (e.g., a 30–70B instruct model)? Would a stronger model told the rule reach about 100%, which would remove the effect?
3. **P1 and the skeleton layout.** If the oracle skeleton lays out the calls in reverse mention order, do same-step slots follow the mention order or the layout? This would show whether P1 is about the request or about the skeleton.
4. **Swap and set scoring.** In the swap condition, 108 of the 111 correct requests are "correct only as a reordered set" (`review_numbers.md`). Is it right that set scoring hides most length-induced rebinding? If so, this strengthens P3 and deserves one sentence.
5. **Turn-taking duplicates.** On ordered BFCL requests, Dream's turn-taking teams duplicate a call in 1 of 104 requests (`agents_bfcl.md`). Should contribution (ii) say "avoids" rather than "prevents"?
6. **Inference methods.** Why report McNemar p ≥ 0.125 as "not significantly different" when the paired bootstrap interval excludes zero (e.g., position versus canvas, 3.8 [1.0, 7.7])? Please report both, or report an equivalence margin.
7. **Equal-length versus team-symmetric.** Please state that equal-length includes requests with no repeated argument. How do Table 1's "Set acc. (eq.)" rows look when restricted to requests that actually have a sibling group?
8. **Missing sources.** Where do 59.3%, 1,076/1,464 and 965/1,014 (Sec. 6) come from in the supplement?
9. **Placeholders.** What exactly do Qwen2.5 position agents see in the other calls' slots? Did you try a neutral placeholder that cannot be copied as a value?

## Scores

- **Overall: 5 (borderline reject).** The empirical work is careful and honest, and the attribution message for dLLM agent evaluation is useful. But for AAMAS, the multi-agent part is thin and its key claims rest on one prompt wording at 7B. The paper is also hard going for a general reader. A clearer multi-agent framing (W1, W7), a format-versus-content control (W2) and the clarity fixes below would move me to 6–7.
- **Confidence: 3.** I am not an expert in diffusion LMs. I checked the numbers against the summaries and the supplement.
- **Originality: 4.** Using a dLLM's decoding schedule as a controllable information structure for parallel deciders is new to me. Most single-span length biases are known (and the authors say so).
- **Significance: 3.** High for dLLM-agent evaluation practice; moderate to low for AAMAS's multi-agent core.
- **Soundness: 3.** Well controlled within its scope. The oracle skeleton, one prompt wording, the format confound and the "no significant difference" phrasing (W2, W4) limit what the claims support.
- **Clarity: 3.** Excellent figures and precise prose, but very dense, with about ten key terms used before definition and several inconsistent terms.
