\section{Designing and Evaluating dLLM Agents}
\label{sec:design}

An agent in deployment must give every slot a length before it knows the value. We tested two ways to cope with this.

\paragraph{A bias toward closing.} Adding $\beta$ to the logits of the closing and padding tokens in every slot rescues large surpluses: with eight extra masks per slot, \dream's set accuracy at $k=4$ rises from 57.0\% to 72.3\% at $\beta=2$, against 87.0\% with exact lengths. It does not rescue near misses: with two extra masks, set accuracy rises only from 2.8\% to 13.8\% at $\beta=8$. We chose $\beta$ on the evaluation requests, so 72.3\% is optimistic.

\paragraph{Lengths from the model.} We give every slot as many masks as its type allows, run one forward pass, read the probability of a closing token at each position as the probability that the value ends there if it has not ended before, and take the most likely length. Our estimate is exact for 51.6\% of \dream's slots and 72.8\% of \llada's, but all of a request's slots are exact in only 14.5\% and 27.0\% of the requests. With these lengths and one token per step, \dream reaches 42.3\% set accuracy and \llada 39.0\%, against 89.8\% and 92.0\% with exact lengths. \dream's wrong estimates are mostly too long (91.5\%, by a median of 7 tokens), where a closing bias could help (we did not combine the two); \llada's are as often too short, which no bias repairs.

\paragraph{Recommendations.} The interface writes the cues that the deciders read, the protocol decides who moves when, and the evaluation decides what is blamed.
(1)~\emph{Interface.} Avoid near misses: a slot one or two tokens too long is the worst choice, and a slot too short cannot be recovered. Let the model write each value's closing token, so that strings can end early, and let slots accept the filler and format tokens a model writes into surplus masks, removing them when decoding (with one extra mask: \tbd{} set accuracy, against 15.5\%).
(2)~\emph{Protocol.} A confidence threshold lets ordered calls move together (89.0\% with 1.9 forward passes per request) and symmetric calls one at a time, because two symmetric choices are rarely confident at once (Section~\ref{sec:symmetric}). In our 7B teams, turn-taking, or one model filling a shared skeleton, avoided the collisions that a number or a rule in the prompt, or sampling, did not prevent.
(3)~\emph{Evaluation.} Report the cost of parallelism under the agent's own length policy, and the errors that remain with one token per step separately (Section~\ref{sec:masquerade}).
