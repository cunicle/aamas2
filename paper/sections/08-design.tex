\section{Designing and Evaluating dLLM Agents}
\label{sec:design}

An agent in deployment must give every slot a length before it knows the value. We tested two ways to cope with this (Table~\ref{tab:mitigation}).

\paragraph{A bias toward closing.} Adding $\beta$ to the logits of the closing and padding tokens in every slot rescues large surpluses: with eight extra masks per slot, \dream's set accuracy at $k=4$ rises from 57.0\% to 72.3\% at $\beta=2$, still below the 87.0\% it reaches with exact lengths. It does not rescue near misses: with two extra masks, set accuracy stays at 2.8\% at $\beta=2$ and reaches only 13.8\% at $\beta=8$, a bias that ruins the large surplus: many slots close at their first position and stay empty, and with eight extra masks only 37.3\% of the output parses. No single bias serves both cases. This matches the probe of Section~\ref{sec:length}: right after the value, the model is far less willing to close with one or two masks left than with eight, so a bias strong enough to close the near misses also closes many slots before their value starts.

\paragraph{Lengths from the model.} The agent can also ask the model. We give every slot as many masks as its type allows, run one forward pass, read the probability of a closing token at each position of a slot as the hazard that its value ends there, and take the most likely length. CAL~\cite{liu2026cal} searches for the length of a single infilled span with 11--18 extra forward passes and leaves several spans to future work; its search may well be more accurate than our estimate, but we did not run it. Our estimate is exact for 51.6\% of \dream's slots and 72.8\% of \llada's, but all of a request's slots are exact in only 14.5\% and 27.0\% of the requests. With these lengths and one token per step, \dream reaches 42.3\% set accuracy and \llada 39.0\%, against 89.8\% and 92.0\% with exact lengths, and committing more tokens per step costs more than with exact lengths (32.3\% for \dream at $k=16$). The two models miss differently. \dream's estimates are mostly too long, and by a median of 7 tokens; only 4.8\% of its slots are one or two tokens too long. This is the regime in which a closing bias helps, but we did not run the two together. \llada's estimates are about as often too short (14.7\%) as too long (12.6\%), and a slot that is too short cannot hold its value at all.

\paragraph{Recommendations.} The interface writes the signals that the slot-level deciders read, the protocol decides who moves when, and the evaluation decides what is blamed.
(1)~\emph{Interface: avoid near misses.} A slot one or two tokens too long is the worst choice and a slot too short cannot be recovered. An agent that cannot know its lengths should leave generous room and bias the slots toward closing; with eight extra masks and $\beta=2$ this reached 72.3\%, against 87.0\% with exact lengths.
(2)~\emph{Protocol: let ordered calls move together and symmetric calls take turns.} When the request orders the calls, parallel commits are nearly free: the confidence threshold keeps \dream at 89.0\% with 1.9 forward passes per request (Section~\ref{sec:ordered}). When nothing tells the calls apart, same-step commits collide, one token per step avoids the collisions, and block-wise decoding nearly does (Section~\ref{sec:symmetric}).
(3)~\emph{Evaluation: control the lengths first.} Only the difference between parallel commits and one token per step, under the same controlled lengths, is a cost of parallelism (Section~\ref{sec:masquerade}).
