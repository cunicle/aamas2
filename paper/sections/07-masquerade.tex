\section{Length Errors Masquerade as Coordination Failures}
\label{sec:masquerade}

\input{tables/masquerade}

The worry about parallel decoding predicts particular errors: a value bound to the wrong call, a duplicated call, a value spliced from two calls. Wrong slot lengths produce exactly these errors with one token per step, where no two decisions are ever made at once (Table~\ref{tab:masquerade}).

\paragraph{The length outranks the order.} A swap sets two cues against each other: the mention order points each slot to its own value, the length to a sibling's. \dream follows the length about 15 times as often as the order (79.4\% against 5.4\% of swapped slots), and \llada about 4 times as often (47.5\% against 12.5\%, on 34 requests). With one token per step this is how single deciders weigh their cues, not a failure to coordinate, but its result looks like one: the rotated sibling group follows the length while the other arguments of the call follow the order, so the arguments of one call refer to different entities (Figure~\ref{fig:example}). Where a whole group rotates together, the turn stays correct as a set: 108 of the 111 swap requests that \dream gets right are right only as a reordered set of calls.

\paragraph{What an evaluator sees.} With the lengths that the model estimates itself (Section~\ref{sec:design}), the condition closest to deployment, the two factors separate cleanly. \dream's \ccer is 1.3\% with one token per step and 2.0\% with 16 when the lengths are exact, and 7.5\% and 11.0\% when they are estimated. Estimated lengths add 6.3 and 9.0 points, parallel commits 0.8 and 3.5. One token per step with estimated lengths thus has about four times the cross-call errors of 16 tokens per step with exact lengths (difference 5.5 points, 95\% interval [3.0, 8.5]; \llada: 14.0\% against 3.0\%, [4.0, 19.0]). Chimera values, which splice individually likely tokens of two values into one (ParallelBench's ``New City'' from ``New York'' and ``Mexico City'', which it attributes to parallel decoding~\cite{kang2026parallelbench}), appear one token per step: in 3.0\% of \dream's requests with one extra mask and 7.0\% of \llada's, against none at $k=16$ with exact lengths. \llada writes ``HSBC Fargo'' into the first call of Figure~\ref{fig:example}. With eight extra masks, \llada duplicates a call in 11.0\% of its requests and binds a value to the wrong call in 26.0\%; \dream with eight extra masks is the exception, with as few cross-call errors as with exact lengths (1.8\% against 2.0\%).

\paragraph{What the wrong actions would do.} Executed, the calls would do harm: with one extra mask and one token per step, 33.3\% of \dream's turns would pass a number off by a factor of ten or more (none with exact lengths); with its own length estimates, 19.8\% would.

\paragraph{An evaluation protocol.} These results concern skeleton interfaces; we do not claim that the errors reported for other dLLM agents~\cite{lu2026bitter,kang2026parallelbench} come from slot lengths. But in a skeleton interface, an error that persists with one token per step is not an error of parallel decoding. Before attributing a dLLM agent's cross-call errors to parallel commits, an evaluator should decode the same requests with one token per step and with controlled slot lengths (exact, swapped, and surplus), and attribute to parallelism only the difference that remains.
