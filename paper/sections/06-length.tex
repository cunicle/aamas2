We now turn to the interface. Sections~\ref{sec:ordered} and~\ref{sec:symmetric} gave every slot exactly as many masks as its reference value has tokens. An agent in deployment cannot do this, because it does not know the values before it decodes them.

\paragraph{One extra mask.} With one token per step, one extra mask per slot lowers \dream's set accuracy from 89.8\% to 15.5\% and \llada's from 92.0\% to 16.0\% (on its 100 requests; Figure~\ref{fig:surplus}a,b). The share of \dream's slots that hold their own value falls from 96.0\% to 37.5\%, and 44.4\% hold their own value followed by more (Table~\ref{tab:length}). In Figure~\ref{fig:example}, HSBC becomes ``HSBC Bank'' and the loan of \$500{,}000 one of \$5{,}000{,}000; four extra masks make them ``HSBC Holdings, Plc'' and \$5{,}000{,}000{,}000. On the same scale, committing 16 tokens per step instead of one costs 5.3 points of set accuracy, and one extra mask costs 74.3. Near misses hurt most: set accuracy is lowest with two extra masks (3.3\%) and recovers with eight (60.0\%; \llada: 38.0\%). The probe in Figure~\ref{fig:surplus}c shows why: with one or two masks left after the value, \dream gives a closing token only 1.0\% and 3.3\% of its probability, and with eight, 74.0\%.

\paragraph{The closing delimiter.} In this interface, ending a value early means writing a closing token right before the one the skeleton has already written, a pattern the models have not seen in training. To separate this from reading the mask count, we move the closing token into the slot: the skeleton no longer writes it, the slot gets one more position, and after the model's closing token the slot is filled with spaces, which leaves ordinary JSON (Table~\ref{tab:length}, middle; dashed in Figure~\ref{fig:surplus}). The delimiter accounts for about half of the effect on the slots. With one extra mask, the share of slots that hold their own value falls from 89.9\% to 62.5\% instead of from 96.0\% to 37.5\%, and the probability of closing with one extra mask rises from 1.0\% to 43.0\%. The other half remains: 26.2\% of the slots still overfill, set accuracy is still lowest with two extra masks (70.3\%, 26.8\%, 16.8\%, and 39.5\% for $s=0,1,2,8$), and numbers still rarely close early (integers with 19.3\% at one extra mask, against 80.8\% when only the closing position is left), so an extra digit still multiplies an amount by ten. S$^3$~\cite{xiong2026s3} reports the same over-generation for schema fields with ample masks. The variant is no remedy as it stands: \dream often fails to close a value or closes it with a merged token such as \texttt{\}\}}, so 7.6\% of its slots never close, and set accuracy with exact lengths drops to 70.3\%.

\paragraph{Swapped lengths and one longer slot.} Rotating the slot lengths within each sibling group gives every changed slot the length of a sibling's value. With one token per step, 79.4\% of \dream's swapped slots then hold the sibling value that fits, both when the slot becomes longer than its own value, which would fit and could close early (79.1\%), and when it becomes shorter (79.7\%). With the closing token in the slot, 49.2\% still do. The swap could also act through taking turns: a slot made too short cannot hold its own value, and a longer slot that moves after it may avoid the value it took. When only one slot of a group is lengthened and its sibling keeps an exact slot, the longer slot takes the sibling's value in 35.4\% of cases, more often than its own (21.8\%), while the sibling keeps its own value in 90.9\%. The two calls then share a value, which no avoidance of duplicates explains.

\paragraph{A single agent obeys it too.} The signal needs no shared canvas. With exact lengths on the 165 swap requests, where sibling slots differ in length, anonymous \dream agents that act at the same time reach 48.5\% set accuracy, against 0.6\% for \qwen agents, whose decoding ignores the mask count (difference 47.9 points, [40.0, 55.8]), and 0\% on the team-symmetric requests, where the agents that call the same tool see the same skeleton (Table~\ref{tab:teams}). The slot length assigns their roles. With swapped lengths, 89.1\% of the swapped slots of agents that act at the same time take the sibling value that fits, and 84.2\% of those of agents that take turns and see the calls already made (Table~\ref{tab:length}, bottom).

\pred{3} holds. A decider reads its mask count as the length of its value. A closing delimiter written after the slot makes this signal binding, but the signal is there without it, and a single agent obeys it as the slots of one canvas do.
