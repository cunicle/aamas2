\section{Symmetric Requests: Simultaneous Deciders Collide}
\label{sec:symmetric}

We now take the order away. A \emph{choose-N} request asks for the weather in $n\in\{2,3,4\}$ cities without saying which call takes which city, either from a list (``\dots any three of these cities: Boston, Dallas, Toronto, Austin, Chicago and Paris'', $n+3$ cities; 60 requests) or openly (``\dots three different US cities of your choice''; 45 requests). Any $n$ different allowed cities are correct, so the deciders must spread over the candidates, as in a dispersion game~\cite{grenager2002dispersion}. Every city slot holds one token, so each call makes a single decision; in open requests this token also limits which cities can be named, so there we report only duplicates. At 16 tokens per step, every city slot moves in the first step from the all-masked canvas. Each slot is then an isolated decider that sees the whole skeleton but none of the other choices: the information a team of agents would have if every agent saw the full plan.

\paragraph{One canvas.} Same-step slots collide (Table~\ref{tab:teams}, right). \dream at 16 tokens per step duplicates a city in every open request and in 15.0\% of list requests; pair by pair, 70.0\% of the open pairs and 5.5\% of the list pairs take the same city. With one token per step, or left to right, no request has a duplicate, and neither with the confidence threshold: two city slots move together only in 8 list requests, where each is confident about a different city; otherwise no slot reaches 0.9 and the slots move one at a time. \llada's calls take turns block by block and rarely collide (at most 4.4\% of open requests; 2 of its 76 same-block, same-step pairs). The AR agent never collides.

\paragraph{Teams of agents.} Anonymous agents that act at the same time receive identical inputs and make identical calls in every team, a sanity check. Agents that take turns never collide. Agents that act at the same time collide whether or not they are numbered: numbered \qwen agents take the same city in every list team, and numbered \dream agents in 78.3\% (numbering lowers the duplicates by 21.7 points, [11.7, 31.7]). Told the convention, \qwen and \dream agents still collide in 70.0\% and 45.0\% of list teams, and take the cities in list order in only 8.3\% and 10.0\%. What the agents share is primary salience, the choice each finds salient for itself, rather than a focal point chosen to match the others~\cite{mehta1994salience}: every \qwen agent that acts at the same time takes the city listed first. Sampling does not break the symmetry. Independent uniform choices among the listed cities would collide in 43.2\% of list requests (20.0\%, 44.4\%, and 65.0\% for $n=2,3,4$). At $T=0.7$, with a separate seed for every agent, teams that act at the same time collide in 75.0--100\% of list runs, while \dream's same-step slots collide in 17.3\%. LLMs tend to choose alike~\cite{ballestero2026monoculture}: what pure coordination rewards, a dispersion game punishes.

\paragraph{What carries over.} The protocol carries over from slots to agents. Deciders that move at the same time collide when nothing orders the choices (in 97.8--100\% of open requests, for \dream's same-step slots and the greedy teams), and deciders that take turns do not. The role carries over only when it is a position on the shared skeleton. \dream's same-step slots differ in 94.5\% of list pairs although they see the same canvas, so their positions change the shared policy's output, though not by list order (11.7\% of requests). A number in an agent's prompt, or a stated rule, leaves most simultaneous agents colliding. \pred{2} holds for the protocol, and the numbered agents fail it as they fail \pred{1}.
