\section{Symmetric Requests: Simultaneous Agents Collide}
\label{sec:symmetric}

We now take the order away. A \emph{choose-N} request asks for the weather in $n\in\{2,3,4\}$ cities without saying which call takes which city, either from a list of $n+3$ one-token cities (``\dots any three of these cities: Boston, Dallas, Toronto, Austin, Chicago and Paris''; 60 requests) or openly (``\dots three different US cities of your choice''; 45 requests). Any $n$ different allowed cities are correct, so the deciders must spread over the candidates, as in a dispersion game~\cite{grenager2002dispersion}. Every city slot holds one token, so each call makes a single decision. At 16 tokens per step, every city slot moves in the first step from the all-masked canvas: each slot sees the whole skeleton but none of the other choices. We test \pred{2} on the list requests; the open ones serve as illustrations, since greedy decoding gives one to ten distinct outputs per $n$ across their paraphrases, and the one-token slot cuts many names (34.1\% of \dream's cities at one token per step, 74.1\% of \qwen's).

\paragraph{One canvas.} \dream's same-step slots duplicate a city in 15.0\% of list requests and 5.5\% of pairs, fewer than independent uniform choices would (43.2\% of requests: 20.0\%, 44.4\%, and 65.0\% for $n=2,3,4$). Their positions set them apart more often than chance, though not in list order (11.7\% of requests). With one token per step, left to right, or the confidence threshold, no request has a duplicate: under the threshold, two city slots move together in only 8 list requests, where each is confident about a different city, and in every other step at most one slot exceeds 0.9, so the slots move one at a time. \llada's calls take turns block by block and rarely collide (2 of its 76 same-block, same-step pairs), and the AR agent never collides. In every open request, at least two of \dream's same-step slots write Chicago.

\paragraph{Teams of agents.} Anonymous agents that act at the same time make identical calls in every team, as their inputs are identical. Agents that take turns never collide. Agents that act at the same time collide whether or not they are numbered: numbered \qwen agents take the same city in every list team and numbered \dream agents in 78.3\% (numbering lowers the duplicates by 21.7 points, [11.7, 31.7]). Told the rule, \qwen and \dream agents still collide in 70.0\% and 45.0\% of list teams and take the cities in list order in only 8.3\% and 10.0\%, although the rule's wording, which refers to the order of the calls, may fit a list of cities poorly. What the agents share is primary salience, the choice each finds salient for itself, rather than a choice made to match the others~\cite{mehta1994salience,bardsley2010explaining}: with greedy decoding, every anonymous or numbered \qwen agent that acts at the same time takes the city listed first, and 55.0\% of those told the rule do. The canvas of Section~\ref{sec:ordered} fits the same description: its slots follow the mention order because the shared policy prefers that order, and the skeleton lays the calls out in it. Sampling does not break the symmetry: at $T=0.7$, with a separate seed for every agent, teams that act at the same time collide in 75.0--100\% of list runs (99.0--99.8\% of \qwen's agents still take the first city), while \dream's same-step slots collide in 17.3\%. LLMs tend to choose alike~\cite{ballestero2026monoculture}: what pure coordination rewards, a dispersion game punishes. Position agents, which see all $n$ city slots, collide in \tbd{} of \qwen's list teams; \dream's choose exactly as its canvas at 16 tokens per step does, since they see the same canvas.

\paragraph{What carries over.} \pred{2} holds for teams of 7B agents: a number in the prompt, a stated rule, or sampling leaves 45--100\% of simultaneous list teams colliding, while agents that take turns never collide. For one canvas it holds only in part: a position on the skeleton sets same-step slots apart more often than chance, and the threshold lets symmetric choices move one at a time.
