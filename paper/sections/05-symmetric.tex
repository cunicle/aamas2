\section{Symmetric Requests: Same-Step Slots Collide When Nothing Orders the Choices}
\label{sec:symmetric}

We now take the focal point away. A \emph{choose-N} request asks for the weather in $n\in\{2,3,4\}$ cities without saying which call takes which city, either from a list (``\dots any three of these cities: Boston, Dallas, Toronto, Austin, Chicago and Paris'', $n+3$ cities; 60 requests) or openly (``\dots three different US cities of your choice''; 45 requests). Any $n$ different allowed cities are correct, so the calls must spread over the candidates, as players of a dispersion game must~\cite{grenager2002dispersion}. Every city slot holds one token, so each call makes a single decision. This token also limits which cities an open request can name, so for open requests we report only duplicates.

\paragraph{One canvas.} Slots that move together collide (Table~\ref{tab:choose}, top). With 16 tokens per step, all of \dream's city slots move in the first step, and two calls take the same city in every open request and in 15.0\% of list requests (10.0\% at $k=2$). With one token per step, no request has a duplicate; with sampling at $T=0.7$, 0.4\% of open runs do. Pair by pair at $k=16$, 70.0\% of the open pairs of slots and 5.5\% of the list pairs take the same city. Collisions also propagate: at $k=2$, each of the 56 pairs that collide although they moved in different steps follows a same-step collision, the later slot repeating a city that two earlier slots already share. \llada's calls take turns block by block and rarely collide (at most 4.4\% of open requests). Even the 76 pairs of its slots that share a block and move in the same step collide only twice, so block-wise turns are not the whole explanation. The AR agent writes its calls in turn and never collides.

\paragraph{Teams of agents.} To test whether same-step slots act like agents that cannot see each other, we give the same requests to teams of $n$ agents, each making one call through the same one-call skeleton (Table~\ref{tab:choose}, bottom). Every agent receives the request followed by a note on the protocol: the agents act at the same time or in turns, seeing the calls made so far, and they are anonymous (``You are one of $n$ assistants'') or numbered (``You are assistant $i$ of $n$''); the supplementary material gives the full texts. Anonymous agents that act at the same time receive identical inputs and, decoding greedily, make identical calls in every team, a sanity check. Agents that take turns never collide, with either model and in either variant. Agents that act at the same time collide whether or not they are numbered. Numbered \qwen agents take the same city in every list team. Numbering lowers \dream's duplicates from 100\% to 78.3\% of list teams (95\% interval of the difference [11.7, 31.7] points), but most numbered teams still collide (55.0\% with two agents, 95.0\% with four). This contradicts our prediction that a number and a list would let agent $i$ take the $i$-th listed city: only 3.3\% of \dream's numbered teams and none of \qwen's take the cities in list order. Instead the agents share a focal point; every \qwen agent that acts at the same time takes the city listed first. Sampling does not break the symmetry. At $T=0.7$, with a separate seed for every agent, teams that act at the same time still collide in 75.0--100\% of list runs and 93.8--100\% of open runs, and 99.8\% of the anonymous \qwen agents still take the first city. LLMs tend to make the same choice as each other~\cite{ballestero2026monoculture}: what pure coordination games reward~\cite{mehta1994salience}, a dispersion game punishes.

\paragraph{What carries over.} The protocol carries over from the agents to the slots. Deciders that move at the same time collide when nothing orders the choices (in 97.8--100\% of open requests, slots and agents alike), and deciders that take turns do not. The role label does not carry over. A number in an agent's prompt leaves most simultaneous agents colliding, whereas a slot's position on the shared canvas separates same-step slots whenever the request lists the candidates: at $k=16$, 94.5\% of \dream's list pairs take different cities although they move in the same step (15.0\% of list requests collide on \dream's canvas against 78.3\% for numbered \dream agents; difference 63.3 points, [51.7, 75.0]). Same-step slots are thus not anonymous agents but simultaneous deciders with a working role label, and they collide like agents that cannot talk when the label has nothing to map onto. \pred{2} holds for the protocol and fails for the agents' labels.
