\section{Ordered Requests: Same-Step Commits Coordinate on Mention Order}
\label{sec:ordered}

We first test \pred{1} on the BFCL requests with exact slot lengths (Table~\ref{tab:ksweep}).

\paragraph{Committing many tokens per step costs little.} Committing 16 tokens per step instead of one lowers \dream's set accuracy from 89.8\% to 84.5\% (by 5.3 points, 95\% interval [3.3, 7.5]) and \llada's from 87.8\% to 79.8\%, while cutting the forward passes per request by factors of 13 and 7. The confidence threshold, which lets every confident position move at once, keeps \dream at 89.0\% with 1.9 forward passes per request, fewer than with $k=16$; the 0.8-point loss is not significant ($p=0.45$). Cross-call errors stay rare at every $k$: at most 2.0\% of requests for \dream and 4.5\% for \llada, against 1.3\% for the AR agent. Between $k=1$ and $k=16$, \dream's rate does not change significantly ($p=0.38$); \llada's rises from 2.5\% to 4.5\% ($p=0.02$). At the slot level, 95.4\% of \dream's slots hold their own value with one token per step and 92.0\% with 16.

\paragraph{The calls are not told apart by their lengths.} With exact lengths, sibling slots of different lengths could be told apart by their length alone, which would keep cross-call errors low without any coordination. On the requests in which every sibling group has slots of equal length (213 for \dream, 216 for \llada), the cross-call error rate stays as low: from 1.9\% to 2.8\% for \dream and from 0.9\% to 1.9\% for \llada between $k=1$ and $k=16$.

\paragraph{Mention order is the focal point.} Consider the length-symmetric requests of the \texttt{parallel} category, in which several calls of one tool differ only in their entities. There, the $i$-th call takes the $i$-th mentioned entity in 95.0--97.5\% of \dream's and 93.8--96.3\% of \llada's requests, with no trend in $k$. Each slot can work out which entity is its own from the request and its own position, so it does not need to see what its siblings commit. Because BFCL lists the reference calls in mention order and our skeleton lays the calls out in that order, this convention is also the correct one.

\paragraph{Where the cost of parallelism lies.} The cost falls mostly inside single values, not between calls. \dream gets 62 requests wrong at $k=16$ against 41 at $k=1$. Output that no longer parses grows from 4 to 21 requests: tokens of one value committed together that do not fit together. Requests with cross-call errors grow only from 5 to 8. \llada's calls take turns at any $k$, because its sibling slots almost never share a block (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). Of its 32 additional failures, 18 are single-call value errors, 8 cross-call errors, and 6 output that does not parse.

When the request orders its entities, simultaneous moves need no communication: the order is a convention that every slot shares, as \pred{1} predicts.
