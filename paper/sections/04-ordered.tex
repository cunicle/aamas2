We first test \pred{1} on the BFCL requests with exact slot lengths (Table~\ref{tab:ksweep}).

\paragraph{Same-step slots coordinate.} At 16 tokens per step, \dream commits the first tokens of 83.0\% of its sibling slot pairs in the same step, so most siblings choose their entities without seeing each other. This lowers \dream's set accuracy only from 89.8\% to 84.5\% (by 5.3 points, 95\% interval [3.3, 7.5]) while cutting the forward passes per request by a factor of 13. The confidence threshold commits 93.7\% of sibling pairs together and keeps 89.0\% with 1.9 forward passes per request ($p=0.45$). On the \texttt{parallel} requests with equal lengths, where lengths cannot tell the calls apart, the $i$-th call takes the $i$-th mentioned entity in 94.0\% of requests with one token per step and in 88.1\% with 16; among the outputs that parse with every call matched, the share stays between 95.0\% and 96.1\% at every $k$. The cost of parallelism lies inside single values: of the 21 requests that \dream gets right with one token per step and wrong with 16, 13 have only single-call errors and 5 no longer parse, against 3 with cross-call errors, and its cross-call error rate rises only from 1.0\% to 2.0\% ($p=0.13$). \llada, whose calls take turns block by block, loses 8.0 points [5.3, 10.8], also mostly in single values. The slots rarely face a hard choice: most values are copied from the request, and our skeleton lays the calls out in the reference order, which BFCL usually gives in mention order.

\paragraph{Simultaneous agents do not.} On the 104 team-symmetric requests, the agents that call the same tool see the same skeleton, so only their prompts can set them apart (Table~\ref{tab:teams}, left). Anonymous agents that act at the same time receive identical inputs and make identical calls, so no team is correct. Numbering raises \dream's agents to 28.8\% and leaves \qwen's at 0\%, against 81.7\% for \dream's canvas at 16 tokens per step on the same requests (difference 52.9 points, [42.3, 62.5]). Told the rule, they reach 48.1\% and 34.6\%; most numbered agents take the entity mentioned first (67.8\% of \dream's and 88.3\% of \qwen's agents in sibling groups). The team agents see only their own call, but this does not explain the gap: on the 86 requests with one tool, the whole skeleton is $n$ copies of one call's skeleton, so a slot knows little more than a numbered agent: its index among $n$ identical calls and, for most sibling pairs, not the other's choice. There the canvas reaches 81.4\%, numbered agents 32.6\% (difference 48.8 points, [38.4, 59.3]), and agents told the rule 55.8\%. Position agents, which see the whole skeleton but none of the other agents' values and fill their own call, close the gap for \dream: they reach 85.6\%, 56.7 points above its numbered agents ([47.1, 66.3]) and not significantly different from its canvas. \qwen's reach 54.8\%, above its agents told the rule (difference 20.2 points, [8.7, 31.7]), although 43 of its 289 agents copy the placeholder of an earlier call into their own. For the same model and the same lack of information about the others, a role given as a position works and a role given as a number does not. Agents that take turns do not differ significantly from one agent that makes all calls: \dream's teams reach 84.6--85.6\% against 88.5\% for its canvas with one token per step, and \qwen's 88.5\% against 91.3\%; each team loses 3 or 4 requests that the one agent gets right and gains at most one ($p\geq0.125$).

In these tests \pred{1} holds for same-step slots and for position agents: a position on the shared skeleton acts as a role, and the request's order assigns the entities to roles. For 7B agents that act at the same time, a number in the prompt, and even a stated rule, is a weak role. Taking turns comes within 4 points of one agent that makes all calls.
