\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Beyond one agent.} Nothing in the model of Section~\ref{sec:setup} depends on diffusion: parallel deciders that cannot see each other coordinate when a shared order assigns roles to positions in a shared structure, collide when roles are symmetric unless they take turns, and obey unintended signals of the structure, such as slot lengths. Multi-agent LLM systems in which workers claim subtasks of a shared plan at the same time~\cite{mao2026delm,rodionov2025hogwild} face the same cases. Our teams suggest that such systems cannot rely on role names or rules in the prompt, or on sampling, to keep simultaneous workers apart, and that turn-taking, or a shared structure that gives each worker its place, is safer. We tested 7B-scale models, one prompt wording per protocol, and single-turn requests, so for larger systems this is a hypothesis, not a finding.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The oracle skeleton fixes the number of calls, their tools, and their argument names, so our numbers isolate the values and do not describe a deployed agent: without the skeleton, \dream reaches 75.3\% set accuracy at $k=2$, against 88.0\% with it. Exact lengths come from the reference values tokenized on their own, which can be shorter than in context: lifting the AR agent's cut-off with eight extra masks changes its output in 44 of 400 requests and raises its set accuracy from 86.5\% to 89.0\%. We tested two dLLMs, which differ in architecture, size, and tokenizer, with greedy decoding except on choose-N; \llada ran on subsets of 100 requests (surplus and estimated lengths) and 34 (swap), where a rate near 40\% has a 95\% interval of about $\pm 10$ points (39.0\% with estimated lengths: [30.0, 48.8]) and, on 34 requests, $\pm 16$, and not in its native tool-calling format. Our agents saw a skeleton of their own call only, with one wording per protocol; on BFCL they lacked the view of the whole skeleton that the slots had. The open choose-N variant gives each city one token. We did not run a prompted placeholder baseline in the style of S$^3$~\cite{xiong2026s3}, the length search of CAL~\cite{liu2026cal}, the closing bias with estimated lengths, or a variable-length dLLM such as DreamOn~\cite{wu2026dreamon}.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Parallel deciders that cannot see each other coordinate on what their shared structure gives them. The same-step slots of one dLLM agent follow the request's order through their positions, while LLM agents that act at the same time cannot use a number, or even a rule, in their prompts; taking turns works for both. The structure also sends signals that no one intended: a dLLM decider reads its slot length as the length of its value, so wrong lengths bind values to the wrong calls even one token per step, and the errors look like failures of parallel decoding. Designers should give parallel deciders their roles through structure and get slot lengths right; evaluators should control slot lengths before they blame parallelism.
