\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Beyond one agent.} The protocols and role cues behind \pred{1} and \pred{2} do not depend on diffusion; the length cue behind \pred{3} does, since an AR agent does not see the mask count. Our teams used 7B models, one prompt wording per protocol, and no orchestrator, and each agent saw only its own call. Systems that give their workers an explicit shared plan or skeleton~\cite{mao2026delm,rodionov2025hogwild,ning2024skeleton} resemble our canvas more than our teams; we did not test them, or larger models.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The oracle skeleton fixes the number of calls, their tools, their argument names, and their order, so our numbers isolate the values and do not describe a deployed agent. Without the skeleton, \dream reaches 75.3\% set accuracy at $k=2$; 12.5\% of its outputs do not parse, and its cross-call errors (5.5\% of requests) are mostly omitted, extra, or duplicated calls, which the oracle rules out. Exact lengths come from the reference values tokenized on their own, which can be shorter than in context: lifting the AR agent's cut-off with eight extra masks changes its output in 44 of 400 requests and raises its set accuracy from 86.5\% to 89.0\%. We tested two dLLMs, which differ in architecture, size, and tokenizer, with greedy decoding except on choose-N. \llada ran on subsets of 100 requests (34 for the swap), where a rate near 40\% has a 95\% interval of about $\pm 10$ points ($\pm 16$ on 34 requests), none of its cross-call contrasts survives Holm's correction, and it did not use its native tool-calling format. The open choose-N requests allow only one-token city names. We did not run a prompted placeholder baseline in the style of S$^3$~\cite{xiong2026s3}, the length search of CAL~\cite{liu2026cal}, the closing bias with estimated lengths, or a variable-length dLLM such as DreamOn~\cite{wu2026dreamon}.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Parallel deciders that cannot see each other coordinate on what their shared structure gives them: same-step slots follow the request's order through their positions, simultaneous 7B agents make little use of a number or a rule in their prompts, and taking turns works for both. The structure also sends cues no one intended: wrong slot lengths corrupt values and bind them to the wrong calls even with one token per step, errors that look like failures of parallel decoding. Designers should give parallel deciders their roles through structure and get slot lengths, delimiters, and type masks right; evaluators should control slot lengths before they blame parallelism.
