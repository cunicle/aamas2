\paragraph{Tool-calling agents and parallel generation.} LLM agents interleave reasoning with tool calls~\cite{yao2023react,schick2023toolformer}, and many requests need several calls in one turn. LLMCompiler~\cite{kim2024llmcompiler} plans such calls and executes them in parallel, BFCL~\cite{patil2025bfcl} evaluates parallel calls in dedicated categories, and Skeleton-of-Thought~\cite{ning2024skeleton} has an LLM write the skeleton of an answer and expand its points in parallel. These works parallelize execution or expansion; we study how one model generates a turn's calls in parallel, and how teams split them.

\paragraph{dLLM agents.} Masked diffusion language models~\cite{sahoo2024mdlm,nie2025llada,ye2025dream,bie2025llada2} are being tried as agent backbones. Lu et al.~\cite{lu2026bitter} find that dLLM agents fail at tool calling, often with malformed JSON or wrong parameters, and attribute this to diffusion noise and to parallel decoding that weakens causal dependencies. They do not vary slot lengths or the number of tokens committed per step. With matched fine-tuning, dLLM agents run over 30\% faster than AR agents but are more prone to structured tool-call failures~\cite{zhen2026dllmagent}. Other work overlaps reasoning with tool execution~\cite{zhao2026dllmsearcher}, traces retry loops to confidence-ordered commits~\cite{qiu2026reverse}, or steers a frozen dLLM with AR agents~\cite{khanal2026agents}.

\paragraph{Coordination without communication.} Agents that cannot communicate can still cooperate by reasoning about each other's decision procedures~\cite{genesereth1986cooperation}, use focal points as a coordination heuristic~\cite{fenster1995coordination,kraus2000focal}, or follow conventions designed off-line as social laws~\cite{shoham1995social}. Human focal points have been explained by salience~\cite{mehta1994salience} and by team reasoning over the labels of the options~\cite{sugden1995theory,bardsley2010explaining}; in dispersion games the players must instead spread over the actions~\cite{grenager2002dispersion}. Zero-shot and label-free coordination ask which conventions independently trained partners can share~\cite{hu2020otherplay,treutlein2021new}, and ad hoc teamwork asks agents to cooperate with unfamiliar teammates without pre-coordination~\cite{stone2010adhoc}. Identical anonymous processes cannot break symmetry deterministically~\cite{angluin1980local}, robot soccer teams assign roles by formation position~\cite{stone1999task}, and Dec-POMDPs formalize who sees what~\cite{bernstein2002complexity}. In multi-agent reinforcement learning, sharing parameters across agents with different roles can hurt~\cite{christianos2021scaling}; our deciders share one policy and get their roles only through their inputs. LLMs often find focal points without communicating~\cite{aharon2026tacit}, coordinate less well when they must model their partners~\cite{agashe2025llmcoordination}, and choose alike by default~\cite{ballestero2026monoculture}; multi-agent LLM systems fail, among other reasons, through misalignment between agents~\cite{cemri2025why}, and parallel workers coordinate through a shared attention cache~\cite{rodionov2025hogwild} or a shared context and task queue~\cite{mao2026delm}.

\paragraph{Parallel decoding and length in dLLMs.} Tokens committed in the same step are sampled without their mutual dependencies~\cite{liu2025copula}. Samplers bound the resulting error with a confidence threshold~\cite{wu2026fastdllm} or an entropy bound~\cite{benhamu2025ebsampler}, and ParallelBench~\cite{kang2026parallelbench} documents invalid combinations such as ``New City''. A fixed canvas length is a known limitation: code infilling degrades when the number of masks does not match the completion~\cite{wu2026dreamon}, fixed format anchors truncate content or add redundant content~\cite{han2026dia}, and ample masks for schema fields lead to over-generation~\cite{xiong2026s3}. The remedies size a single span or response: by searching its length~\cite{liu2026cal}, growing or shrinking the canvas~\cite{wu2026dreamon}, estimating where a span ends~\cite{han2026dia}, or reading end-of-sequence signals~\cite{yang2026rhoeos,li2026daedal}. Constrained decoding enforces grammars~\cite{mundler2026cfg,geng2023grammar} or finite automata~\cite{dang2026constrained}; our skeleton is the simplest such constraint. We do not claim to discover the length bias; we measure it in actions with several slots and show that it binds values to the wrong calls.
