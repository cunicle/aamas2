\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Agents increasingly act in parallel. A tool-calling agent answers one request with several calls at once, such as the weather in two cities or a loan check at two banks, and in multi-agent LLM systems several workers claim subtasks of a shared plan at the same time~\cite{mao2026delm,rodionov2025hogwild}. Each parallel decision must then take its own role, such as an entity or a subtask, without seeing what the others take. If two decisions take the same role, an action is duplicated; if they swap roles, actions are bound to the wrong objects. Coordination without communication has well-known answers: a focal point that everyone notices~\cite{schelling1960strategy}, a shared convention~\cite{lewis1969convention}, labels that break the symmetry, or taking turns. We ask which of them work for LLM-based deciders.

Diffusion language models (dLLMs) make this question precise. A dLLM agent can write all the calls of a turn on one canvas and commit many tokens in each denoising step. Tokens committed in the same step are chosen without seeing each other, so the decoding schedule decides which slots move together and what each one sees, and we can vary it exactly. dLLM agents also matter in their own right: they fail at tool calling, and the failures have been blamed on diffusion noise and parallel decoding~\cite{lu2026bitter}. To keep every call well formed, such an agent can write the syntax of its turn in advance as a skeleton and leave only the argument values masked. Each value then gets a slot with a fixed number of masks, set before the value is known. Figure~\ref{fig:example} shows what this does even when the agent commits one token per step: with one extra mask per slot, HSBC becomes ``HSBC Bank'' and a \$500{,}000 loan becomes one of \$5{,}000{,}000; with the slot lengths of the two banks swapped, the banks change calls.

We treat the slots of such a turn as deciders that share one policy and differ only in their position and in what they observe, and we derive predictions from three cues they share: the order in which the request mentions its entities (a focal point), each decider's role (a position on the shared skeleton, or a number in an agent's prompt), and the slot length (a signal). We test them on the parallel calls of the Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard~\cite{patil2025bfcl} and on symmetric requests, with two dLLMs and an AR model, and we give the same requests to teams of LLM agents that each make one call, at the same time or in turns, anonymous, numbered, or told the convention.

We find three things. (1) When the request orders its entities, slots that move together coordinate on that order: committing 16 tokens per step instead of one costs \dream only 5.3 points of set accuracy. Agents that act at the same time cannot use the order: numbered \dream agents reach 28.8\% set accuracy, and 48.1\% when told the convention, against 81.7\% for the same-step slots, while agents that take turns match the single agent. A role given as a position on a shared skeleton coordinates; a role given in a prompt does not. (2) When nothing orders the choices, simultaneous slots and agents collide, sampling does not help, and turn-taking prevents the collisions, as does a confidence threshold that commits uncertain choices one at a time. (3) The strongest cue is the slot length. A decider reads the number of masks as the length of its value: right lengths let anonymous agents that cannot see each other split the calls, and wrong lengths bind values to the wrong calls, even one token per step and for a single agent. A closing delimiter written after each slot doubles the cost of one extra mask, but half of it remains when the model writes the delimiter itself. With slot lengths that the model estimates itself, one token per step produces about four times as many cross-call errors as 16 tokens per step with exact lengths, errors of the kind usually blamed on parallel decoding.

Our contributions are (i) a test bed and a model that separate the protocol, the role cues, and the action interface of parallel deciders (Section~\ref{sec:setup}); (ii) evidence, across ordered and symmetric requests and across one agent and teams of agents, that roles anchored in shared structure coordinate parallel deciders, roles stated in prompts do not, and turn-taking always does (Sections~\ref{sec:ordered} and~\ref{sec:symmetric}); (iii) evidence that slot length acts as a signal that single deciders obey, with a control for the closing delimiter (Section~\ref{sec:length}); and (iv) an attribution protocol and design guidance for dLLM agents (Sections~\ref{sec:masquerade} and~\ref{sec:design}). Length biases of dLLMs are known for single spans~\cite{wu2026dreamon,han2026dia,liu2026cal}; we show what they do to the joint actions of agents.
