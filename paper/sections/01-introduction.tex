\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Agents increasingly act in parallel. A tool-calling agent answers one request with several calls at once, for example to get the weather in two cities or to check a loan at two banks, and in multi-agent large language model (LLM) systems several workers claim subtasks of a shared plan concurrently~\cite{mao2026delm,rodionov2025hogwild}. Whenever their view of each other is delayed or absent, each parallel decision must take its own role, such as an entity or a subtask, without seeing what the others take. If two decisions take the same role, an action is duplicated; if they swap roles, actions are bound to the wrong objects. Agents that cannot communicate have known ways to coordinate: a focal point that all of them notice~\cite{schelling1960strategy,fenster1995coordination}, a shared convention or social law~\cite{lewis1969convention,shoham1995social}, labels that break the symmetry, or taking turns. We ask which of these work for LLM-based deciders.

Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) make this question precise. A dLLM starts from a canvas of mask tokens and, at each denoising step, commits some of them; tokens committed in the same step are chosen without seeing each other. A dLLM agent can write all the calls of a turn on one canvas, so its decoding schedule decides which parts of the turn are chosen together and what each part sees, and we can vary it exactly. dLLM agents also matter in their own right: they often fail at tool calling, and the failures have been blamed on diffusion noise and parallel decoding~\cite{lu2026bitter}. To keep every call well formed, such an agent can write the syntax of its turn in advance as a skeleton and leave only the argument values masked. Each value then gets a slot with a fixed number of masks, set before the value is known. Figure~\ref{fig:example} shows what this does even when the agent commits one token per step. With one extra mask per slot, HSBC becomes ``HSBC Bank'' and a \$500{,}000 loan becomes one of \$5{,}000{,}000; with the slot lengths of the two banks swapped, the banks change calls.

We treat the slots of such a turn as deciders that share one policy and differ only in their position and in what they observe, and we describe who decides together and who sees what in the terms of team decision problems~\cite{radner1962team}. Three cues could set the deciders apart: the order in which the request mentions its entities, each decider's role (a position on the shared skeleton, or a number in an agent's prompt), and the slot length. We test hypotheses about each on the parallel calls of the Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard (BFCL)~\cite{patil2025bfcl} and on symmetric requests that leave the assignment open, with two dLLMs and an autoregressive (AR) model. We give the same requests to teams of 7B LLM agents that each make one call, at the same time or in turns, and anonymous, numbered, or told the rule that agent $i$ makes the $i$-th mentioned call.

We find three things. (1)~When the request orders its entities, slots that decide in the same step take them in that order: committing 16 tokens per step instead of one costs \dream only 5.3 points of set accuracy (the share of requests whose calls are all correct). Simultaneous agents that get their role as a number in the prompt mostly fail (numbered \dream agents: 28.8\%, or 48.1\% when told the rule, against 81.7\% for the slots on the same requests), even where the whole skeleton tells a slot no more than its number; the same agents given their position on the whole skeleton, but none of the others' choices, reach 85.6\%. Agents that take turns come within 4 points of one agent that makes all calls. (2)~When nothing orders the choices, simultaneous agents collide whether numbered, told the rule, or sampled; turn-taking prevents the collisions, and so does a confidence threshold. (3)~Slot length is a strong cue: surplus masks lengthen values, and a slot with a sibling's length often takes that sibling's value, even with one token per step, partly through two interface choices we isolate, a pre-written closing delimiter and a type mask; removing either recovers part of the loss. With the model's own length estimates, one token per step produces more cross-call errors than 16 tokens per step with exact lengths.

Our contributions are:
(i)~a test bed that separates the protocol, the role cues, and the action interface of parallel LLM deciders (Section~\ref{sec:setup});
(ii)~evidence that, on ordered requests, a position on a shared skeleton guides parallel deciders, slots and \dream agents alike, while a number or a rule in a 7B agent's prompt mostly does not, and that turn-taking prevents collisions on ordered and symmetric requests (Sections~\ref{sec:ordered} and~\ref{sec:symmetric});
(iii)~an analysis of slot length as a cue, with controls for the closing delimiter and the type mask (Section~\ref{sec:length}); and
(iv)~an attribution protocol and design guidance for skeleton-based dLLM agents (Sections~\ref{sec:masquerade} and~\ref{sec:design}).
Length biases of dLLMs are known for single spans~\cite{wu2026dreamon,han2026dia,liu2026cal}; we show what they do to the joint actions of an agent.
